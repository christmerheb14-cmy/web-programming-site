Introduction

The Internet is a global system of interconnected computer networks that connects billions of devices worldwide, ranging from personal smartphones to massive server clusters. The Internet functions like a single computer and has constructed a unified digital structure relying on standardized communication rules, mainly TCP/IP. The protocols describe how data is divided into small packets, sent across physical cables and wireless channels, and accurately reassembled at its final location. The Internet is not owned or controlled by a defined entity; it is a decentralized resource used for modern communications, commerce and information sharing. The Internet provides the logical and physical network that enables raw data to be sent. However, services on top such as email, video streaming, and the World Wide Web let you access hyperlinked documents, trade goods, chat with people over long distances, and more.

Internet History Timeline

1969 — ARPANET

It was the first large-scale packet-switching network and the principal predecessor of today’s Internet.

Source: Live Science

1971 — Email was introduced on ARPANET, including the use of @ to separate a user from a computer.

Email became one of the first widely useful applications of computer networking and helped shift networks from resource-sharing systems into communication platforms.

Source: National Science and Media Museum

1973–1974 — TCP was developed by Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn as a method for connecting separate packet-switched networks.

The idea of an “internetwork”—multiple independent networks communicating with one another—was central to creating the Internet.

Source: Live Science

1983 — ARPANET adopted TCP/IP as its standard networking protocol suite.

TCP/IP provided a common way for different networks and computers to communicate. This is widely regarded as a foundational moment for the modern Internet.

Source: Computer History Museum

1983 — The Domain Name System (DNS) was introduced.

DNS translated numerical IP addresses into memorable names such as example.com, making the growing network much easier for people to use.

Source: National Science and Media Museum

1986 — NSFNET went online, linking U.S. supercomputing centers and later connecting regional academic networks.

NSFNET greatly expanded Internet access beyond the original ARPANET research community and helped establish a national Internet backbone.

Source: Live Science

1989 — Tim Berners-Lee proposed the World Wide Web at CERN.

The proposal combined hypertext with networked information, creating a simple system for linking and accessing documents across the Internet.

Source: CERN

1991 — The World Wide Web became publicly available.

CERN released the Web software and made it possible for people outside CERN to create and access Web pages. This marked the beginning of the Web’s global expansion.

Source: CERN

1993 — The Mosaic Web browser was released.

Mosaic made the Web easier to use through a graphical interface and helped bring Internet use to a much broader, nonacademic audience.

Source: CERN

1993 — CERN made the Web software freely available on a royalty-free basis.

Removing licensing barriers encouraged universities, companies, and individuals to build Web servers, browsers, and online services.

Source: CERN

1994–1995 — Commercial Internet and Web use expanded rapidly.

The rise of commercial browsers, Internet service providers, and online businesses helped transform the Internet from primarily an academic and research network into a mass communication and commerce platform.

Source: National Science and Media Museum

Key Technologies

TCP/IP

The core protocols that allow devices and networks to communicate and exchange data across the Internet.

Domain Name System (DNS)

Converts human-readable domain names, such as example.com, into numerical IP addresses that computers use.

HTTP/HTTPS

Protocols used to transfer web pages and other resources between web browsers and servers. HTTPS adds encryption and authentication for safer communication.

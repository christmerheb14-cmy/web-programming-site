Introduction

The Internet is a global system of interconnected computer networks that connects billions of devices worldwide, ranging from personal smartphones to massive server clusters. The Internet functions like a single computer and has constructed a unified digital structure relying on standardized communication rules, mainly TCP/IP.

These protocols describe how data is divided into small packets, sent across physical cables and wireless channels, and accurately reassembled at its final location. The Internet is not owned or controlled by a defined entity; it is a decentralized resource used for modern communications, commerce, and information sharing.

The Internet provides the logical and physical network that enables raw data to be sent. Services on top, such as email, video streaming, and the World Wide Web, let people access hyperlinked documents, trade goods, chat with people over long distances, and more.

History of the Internet

1969 — ARPANET Began Operating

Event: ARPANET began operating, initially connecting four U.S. research institutions.

Why it mattered: It was the first large-scale packet-switching network and the principal predecessor of today’s Internet.

1971 — Email Was Introduced on ARPANET

Event: Email was introduced on ARPANET, including the use of @ to separate a user from a computer.

Why it mattered: Email became one of the first widely useful applications of computer networking and helped shift networks from resource-sharing systems into communication platforms.

1973–1974 — TCP Was Developed

Event: TCP was developed by Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn as a method for connecting separate packet-switched networks.

Why it mattered: The idea of an “internetwork”—multiple independent networks communicating with one another—was central to creating the Internet.

1983 — ARPANET Adopted TCP/IP

Event: ARPANET adopted TCP/IP as its standard networking protocol suite.

Why it mattered: TCP/IP provided a common way for different networks and computers to communicate. This is widely regarded as a foundational moment for the modern Internet.

1983 — The Domain Name System Was Introduced

Event: The Domain Name System (DNS) was introduced.

Why it mattered: DNS translated numerical IP addresses into memorable names such as example.com, making the growing network much easier for people to use.

1986 — NSFNET Went Online

Event: NSFNET went online, linking U.S. supercomputing centers and later connecting regional academic networks.

Why it mattered: NSFNET greatly expanded Internet access beyond the original ARPANET research community and helped establish a national Internet backbone.

History of the World Wide Web

1989 — The World Wide Web Was Proposed

Event: Tim Berners-Lee proposed the World Wide Web at CERN.

Why it mattered: The proposal combined hypertext with networked information, creating a simple system for linking and accessing documents across the Internet.

1991 — The World Wide Web Became Publicly Available

Event: The World Wide Web became publicly available.

Why it mattered: CERN released the Web software and made it possible for people outside CERN to create and access Web pages. This marked the beginning of the Web’s global expansion.

1993 — The Mosaic Web Browser Was Released

Event: The Mosaic Web browser was released.

Why it mattered: Mosaic made the Web easier to use through a graphical interface and helped bring Internet use to a much broader, nonacademic audience.

1993 — CERN Made the Web Software Freely Available

Event: CERN made the Web software freely available on a royalty-free basis.

Why it mattered: Removing licensing barriers encouraged universities, companies, and individuals to build Web servers, browsers, and online services.

1994–1995 — Commercial Internet and Web Use Expanded

Event: Commercial Internet and Web use expanded rapidly.

Why it mattered: The rise of commercial browsers, Internet service providers, and online businesses helped transform the Internet from primarily an academic and research network into a mass communication and commerce platform.

Key Technologies

TCP/IP

The core protocols that allow devices and networks to communicate and exchange data across the Internet.

Domain Name System (DNS)

Converts human-readable domain names, such as example.com, into numerical IP addresses that computers use.

HTTP/HTTPS

Protocols used to transfer web pages and other resources between web browsers and servers. HTTPS adds encryption and authentication for safer communication.
